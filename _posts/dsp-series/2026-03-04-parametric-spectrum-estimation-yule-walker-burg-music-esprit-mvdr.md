## Why Parametric?

Nonparametric methods are limited by the **Rayleigh resolution limit**: two tones closer than $F_s/N$ cannot be separated, no matter how much averaging you do. A parametric method assumes the signal is generated by a model with a few parameters, estimates those parameters, and computes the spectrum from the model. The result can resolve far below the Rayleigh limit.

The trade-off: if the model is wrong, the estimate is wrong — and confidently so.

---

## AR Spectral Estimation

Assume the signal is an AR process of order $p$:

$$x[n]=-\sum_{k=1}^{p}a_kx[n-k]+w[n]$$

The spectrum is

$$\hat P_{AR}(e^{j\omega})=\frac{\hat\sigma_w^2}{\left\vert1+\sum_{k=1}^{p}\hat a_k e^{-j\omega k}\right\vert^2}$$

The denominator creates sharp peaks when the poles approach the unit circle. This is why AR gives high resolution from short records.

### Choosing the Order

| Criterion | Formula | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| AIC | $N\ln\hat\sigma_p^2+2p$ | Tends to overestimate |
| MDL (BIC) | $N\ln\hat\sigma_p^2+p\ln N$ | Consistent, penalizes more |
| CAT | $\frac{1}{N}\sum\dots$ | Similar to AIC |

For a single sinusoid in noise, order 2 per tone plus a few for the noise works well. For speech LPC, $p\approx F_s/1000+2$.

## The Yule–Walker Method

Estimate the autocorrelation from the data, then solve the normal equations:

$$\hat r[l]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1-l}x[n]x[n+l],\qquad l=0,\dots,p$$

$$\begin{bmatrix}\hat r[0]&\hat r[1]&\dots&\hat r[p-1]\\\hat r[1]&\hat r[0]&\dots&\hat r[p-2]\\\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\\hat r[p-1]&\hat r[p-2]&\dots&\hat r[0]\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\hat a_1\\\vdots\\\hat a_p\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\hat r[1]\\\vdots\\\hat r[p]\end{bmatrix}$$

Solve with Levinson-Durbin.

### Properties

**Advantages:**
- Guaranteed stable all-pole model ($\vert k_m\vert<1$ for a valid autocorrelation).
- Computationally efficient ($O(p^2)$).
- Simple to implement.

**Disadvantages:**
- The **biased** autocorrelation estimate (dividing by $N$ regardless of lag) tapers the data, which **biases and broadens** the spectral peaks.
- The **unbiased** estimate can produce an invalid autocorrelation matrix that is not positive definite, and then Levinson-Durbin fails.
- Poor resolution for short records with high SNR, compared with Burg.

---

## The Burg Method

Burg estimates the reflection coefficients directly by minimizing the **forward and backward prediction errors** at each stage, constrained to $\vert k_m\vert<1$.

At stage $m$:

$$\hat k_m=\frac{-2\sum_n e_f^{(m-1)}[n]\,e_b^{(m-1)}[n-1]}{\sum_n\left(\left(e_f^{(m-1)}[n]\right)^2+\left(e_b^{(m-1)}[n-1]\right)^2\right)}$$

Then update the errors with the lattice recursion.

### Properties

**Advantages:**
- **Higher resolution** than Yule–Walker for short records.
- Stable by construction: $\vert k_m\vert<1$ is enforced.
- No autocorrelation estimate, so no windowing bias.
- Uses both forward and backward errors — effectively twice the data.

**Disadvantages:**
- Spectral **peak splitting** at high SNR with long records (the estimated pole splits into two).
- Spectral **line shifting** — the peak position drifts slightly with SNR and record length.
- Slightly more computation than Yule–Walker.

### Yule–Walker vs Burg

| Aspect | Yule–Walker | Burg |
|---|---|---|
| Autocorrelation needed | Yes | No |
| Stability | Guaranteed for valid $r$ | Guaranteed always |
| Resolution | Lower | Higher |
| Peak splitting | No | Yes, at high SNR |
| Line shifting | Small | Present |
| Computation | $O(p^2)$ | $O(pN)$ per stage |

## MA and ARMA Estimation

### MA(q)

$$x[n]=\sum_{k=0}^{q}b_kw[n-k]$$

$$P_{MA}(e^{j\omega})=\sigma_w^2\left\vert\sum_{k=0}^{q}b_ke^{-j\omega k}\right\vert^2$$

The autocorrelation is nonzero only for lags $0\le l\le q$, so the parameters come from the **modified Yule–Walker** equations. MA needs a high order to represent sharp peaks.

### ARMA(p,q)

$$P_{ARMA}(e^{j\omega})=\sigma_w^2\frac{\left\vert\sum_{k=0}^{q}b_ke^{-j\omega k}\right\vert^2}{\left\vert1+\sum_{k=1}^{p}a_ke^{-j\omega k}\right\vert^2}$$

Estimation is **nonlinear**. Standard approaches:

1. **Modified Yule–Walker:** estimate the AR part from the autocorrelation at lags $q+1,\dots,q+p$, then estimate the MA part from the residual.
2. **Durbin's method:** fit a high-order AR, then factor into AR and MA.
3. **Nonlinear least squares / iterative:** best accuracy, most computation.

ARMA fits peaks and notches with fewer parameters than pure AR or MA, but the estimation is harder.

## Pisarenko Harmonic Decomposition

The first subspace method. Assume $M$ exponentials in white noise:

$$x[n]=\sum_{i=1}^{M}A_ie^{j(\omega_in+\phi_i)}+w[n]$$

The autocorrelation matrix $\mathbf{R}_{p}$ of size $(p+1)\times(p+1)$ with $p\ge M$ has eigenvalues:

- $M$ large eigenvalues (signal subspace),
- $p+1-M$ small eigenvalues equal to $\sigma_w^2$ (noise subspace).

The **smallest** eigenvector $\mathbf{v}$ is orthogonal to the signal subspace, and its polynomial

$$V(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{p}v_kz^{-k}$$

has roots on the unit circle at the signal frequencies.

### Properties

- Resolution far beyond the Rayleigh limit.
- Needs only one eigenvector (the smallest).
- Uses only $M$ of the $p$ noise eigenvalues → statistically inefficient.
- Sensitive to noise and to an incorrect $M$.

## MUSIC

**MUltiple SIgnal Classification** uses the whole noise subspace.

### The Step

1. Estimate the $p\times p$ autocorrelation matrix $\hat{\mathbf{R}}$ from the data.
2. Eigendecompose: $\hat{\mathbf{R}}=\mathbf{U}_s\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_s\mathbf{U}_s^H+\mathbf{U}_n\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_n\mathbf{U}_n^H$.
3. The noise subspace $\mathbf{U}_n$ spans the $(p-M)$ smallest eigenvectors.
4. The steering vector at frequency $\omega$ is

$$\mathbf{a}(\omega)=\left[1,\ e^{j\omega},\ e^{2j\omega},\dots,e^{j(p-1)\omega}\right]^T$$

5. The **pseudospectrum** is

$$\hat P_{MUSIC}(e^{j\omega})=\frac{1}{\mathbf{a}^H(\omega)\mathbf{U}_n\mathbf{U}_n^H\mathbf{a}(\omega)}$$

At a true signal frequency, $\mathbf{a}(\omega)$ is orthogonal to the noise subspace, so the denominator is zero and the pseudospectrum peaks sharply.

### Properties

| Aspect | Value |
|---|---|
| Resolution | Far below Rayleigh, approaches the Cramér–Rao bound |
| Consistency | Yes, as $N\to\infty$ |
| Requirement | Correct model order $M$ |
| Cost | Eigendecomposition $O(p^3)$ |
| Weakness | Coherent sources (multipath) decorrelate the matrix and break it |

### Coherent Sources

If two sources are perfectly correlated (multipath), the signal covariance matrix loses rank and MUSIC fails. The fix is **spatial smoothing**: average subarray covariance matrices to restore rank.

## ESPRIT

**Estimation of Signal Parameters via Rotational Invariance Techniques** avoids the search over $\omega$.

### The Rotational Invariance

Split the steering matrix into two overlapping subarrays:

$$\mathbf{U}_s=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{U}_1\\ \text{last row}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\text{first row}\\ \mathbf{U}_2\end{bmatrix}$$

The two subarrays differ by a rotation $\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\operatorname{diag}(e^{j\omega_1},\dots,e^{j\omega_M})$:

$$\mathbf{U}_2=\mathbf{U}_1\boldsymbol{\Phi}$$

Solve in the least-squares sense:

$$\boldsymbol{\Psi}=\left(\mathbf{U}_1^H\mathbf{U}_1\right)^{-1}\mathbf{U}_1^H\mathbf{U}_2$$

The eigenvalues of $\boldsymbol{\Psi}$ are $e^{j\omega_i}$, so the frequencies are read directly from the eigenvalue phases — **no spectral search**.

### MUSIC vs ESPRIT

| Aspect | MUSIC | ESPRIT |
|---|---|---|
| Search | 1-D spectral search over $\omega$ | None — closed form |
| Cost | Eigendecomposition + search | Eigendecomposition + small eigenproblem |
| Accuracy | Slightly better | Slightly worse but close |
| Array requirement | Any | Requires **shift-invariant** structure |
| Coherent sources | Fails without smoothing | Also fails without smoothing |

In practice ESPRIT is preferred when the array is shift-invariant (ULA), because it is much faster and the accuracy difference is small.

## MVDR (Capon) Beamformer

The **minimum variance distortionless response** estimator minimizes output power subject to unity gain at the look frequency:

$$\min_{\mathbf{w}}\mathbf{w}^H\hat{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{w}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\mathbf{w}^H\mathbf{a}(\omega)=1$$

The solution is

$$\mathbf{w}=\frac{\hat{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}\mathbf{a}(\omega)}{\mathbf{a}^H(\omega)\hat{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}\mathbf{a}(\omega)}$$

and the spectrum is

$$\hat P_{MVDR}(e^{j\omega})=\frac{1}{\mathbf{a}^H(\omega)\hat{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}\mathbf{a}(\omega)}$$

### Properties

- Better resolution than AR, worse than MUSIC.
- Does **not** need the model order — no eigendecomposition.
- Robust to coherent sources, unlike MUSIC.
- Requires inverting $\hat{\mathbf{R}}$, which needs $N>p$ and can be ill-conditioned.

| Method | Resolution | Needs order? | Coherent sources | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Periodogram | Rayleigh | No | OK | Low |
| AR (Yule–Walker) | Good | Yes | OK | $O(p^2)$ |
| AR (Burg) | Better | Yes | OK | $O(pN)$ |
| Pisarenko | High | Yes | Fails | $O(p^3)$ |
| MUSIC | Highest | Yes | Fails | $O(p^3)$ + search |
| ESPRIT | Highest | Yes | Fails | $O(p^3)$ |
| MVDR | High | No | OK | $O(p^3)$ |

## Model Order Selection

All subspace methods need $M$, the number of sources. Getting it wrong is the main failure mode.

### Information Criteria

$$\text{AIC}(M)=-2\ln L(\hat\theta_M)+2k_M$$
$$\text{MDL}(M)=-\ln L(\hat\theta_M)+\tfrac12k_M\ln N$$

For the eigenvalue problem, the practical form uses the noise eigenvalues:

$$\text{MDL}(M)=-\ln\left(\frac{\left(\prod_{i=M+1}^{p}\lambda_i\right)^{1/(p-M)}}{\frac{1}{p-M}\sum_{i=M+1}^{p}\lambda_i}\right)^{(p-M)N}+\tfrac12M(2p-M)\ln N$$

Choose the $M$ that minimizes the criterion.

### Practical Rules

- **Eigenvalue gap:** plot the eigenvalues and look for the knee where they drop to the noise floor.
- **Overestimate slightly** rather than underestimate — an extra source adds a spurious peak but does not lose a real one.
- **Coherent sources:** the gap disappears; use spatial smoothing or a different method.
- **Low SNR / short record:** increase $p$ (the matrix size), not just $M$.

---

## Practical Notes

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Spurious peaks | Order too high | Use MDL, not AIC |
| Missed weak source | Order too low | Increase $M$ |
| MUSIC fails on multipath | Coherent sources | Spatial smoothing |
| Burg peak splitting | High SNR, long record | Yule–Walker or reduce order |
| Unstable AR filter | Invalid autocorrelation | Biased estimator or Burg |
| MVDR matrix singular | $N\le p$ | Increase $N$ or diagonal loading |

**Diagonal loading:** add $\epsilon\mathbf{I}$ to $\hat{\mathbf{R}}$ before inverting. It regularizes the solution and makes MVDR robust to steering-vector errors.

---

## Summary

1. Parametric methods beat the Rayleigh limit by fitting a model rather than smoothing data.
2. Yule–Walker is simple and stable but biased; Burg has higher resolution and is always stable but can split peaks.
3. Pisarenko uses the smallest noise eigenvector; MUSIC uses the whole noise subspace and gives a sharp pseudospectrum.
4. ESPRIT reads frequencies from the eigenvalues of a rotation operator — no search needed.
5. MVDR (Capon) needs no model order and handles coherent sources.
6. Model order selection with MDL is the make-or-break step for all subspace methods.
